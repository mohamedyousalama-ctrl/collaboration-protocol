Abstract
Human-AI collaboration challenges persist even when AI systems are technically capable.
We argue these challenges may often stem from Silent Intent Inferencea structural pattern
where AI resolves semantic ambiguity without explicit user authorization. We propose framing
this not as mere interaction friction but as a potential accountability challenge: it may create
oversight gaps, dicult-to-trace decisions, and gradual reduction of user agency.
We introduce the Collaboration Protocol (CP), a theoretical framework for preserving
human semantic authority through four operationalized constructs: (1) Intent Control Degree (ICD), a user-adjustable parameter (0.01.0) governing how ambiguity is resolved; (2)
Intent Pivot Points (IPP), semantic locations where meaning may branch, with a six-type
taxonomy; (3) Intent Fidelity Index (IFI), a composite metric measuring intent preservation
across interactions; and (4) Context Roots (CR), a mechanism for anchoring ambiguity in
interaction history, enabling multi-turn and multi-session intent tracking.
The framework introduces the Semantic Materiality Proxy (SMP) for objective ambiguity classication and the Context Factor (CF) for modeling how prior interactions aect
current ambiguity. Each construct includes specic measurement procedures enabling empirical validation. We propose a phased research agenda: quantifying silent inference prevalence
in current LLM systems, validating ICD eects on collaboration quality, and establishing IPP
annotation reliability. This paper establishes a falsiable theoretical foundationthe constructs
and hypotheses are designed to be empirically tested and potentially refuted by the research
community.
Keywords: Human-LLM Interaction, Silent Intent Inference, Intent Alignment, Semantic Authority, Collaboration Protocol, Human-in-the-Loop AI, Context Roots, AI Governance
1 Introduction
1.1 Motivation
Modern AI systems routinely produce outputs that are technically correct, linguistically coherent,
and aligned with training objectivesyet still wrong for the user. This gap cannot be explained
solely by model alignment, prompt quality, or user error. Instead, it reects a deeper protocollevel failure: AI systems silently resolve ambiguity without verifying human intent. We term this
phenomenon Silent Intent Inference.

1.2 Illustrative Failure Case
Consider the following observed interaction:
User: What is the capital of Egypt?
AI: Cairo
User: How many population?
AI: 112 million
Post-hoc analysis reveals that the AI inferred the second question referred to Egypt (population
∼112 million) rather than Cairo (population ∼22 million), based on conversational proximity rather
than explicit user intent. While this inference was reasonable, it was unauthorized.
This interaction demonstrates four critical phenomena: (1) ambiguity existed in the user's query;
(2) a semantic pivot point occurred where meaning could branch; (3) the AI silently selected an
interpretation without surfacing alternatives; and (4) the output violated user intent despite being
factually defensible.
This pattern generalizes across domains:
Healthcare. A patient asks an AI assistant: What are my treatment options for this condition? The AI provides a comprehensive list of all medical interventions. But the patient wanted
options covered by their insurance, or options with minimal side eects, or options their doctor
had recommended. The AI silently resolved options to mean all technically possible optionsa
reasonable interpretation, but one the patient never authorized.
Legal. A small business owner asks: Is this contract fair? The AI analyzes the terms and
responds armatively. But fair to whom? Fair compared to what standard? The AI assumed the
user wanted a bilateral fairness assessment against industry norms. The user wanted to know if the
contract was fair to them specically, given their weak negotiating position. The silent inference
produced a technically accurate but practically misleading response.
Creative. A user requests: Write a story about a hero. The AI produces a 500-word fantasy
narrative with a male protagonist. But the user imagined a realistic contemporary setting, a female
protagonist, and a 2,000-word length. The AI made silent decisions about genre, tone, length,
character demographics, and narrative structurenone of which the user specied, all of which the
AI resolved without asking.
In each case, the AI performed well by conventional metrics: responses were coherent, relevant,
and grounded in the query. Yet each response failed the user's actual intent. The failures were not
capability failures but protocol failuresthe system lacked a mechanism to surface its interpretive
choices before committing to them.
1.3 Contributions
This paper makes the following contributions:
1. We name and dene Silent Intent Inference as a structural accountability challenge in
human-AI interaction, distinct from model capability limitations.
2. We introduce three operationalized constructsIntent Control Degree (ICD), Intent Pivot
Points (IPP), and Intent Fidelity Index (IFI)each with specic measurement protocols.
3. We propose seven Protocol Laws governing intent-preserving interaction between humans and
AI systems.
4. We provide falsiable hypotheses and ready-to-run validation studies, enabling the research community to empirically test the framework's claims.

1.4 Scope and Boundaries
This paper establishes a theoretical framework; it does not report empirical ndings. The proposed
constructs are operationalizable and testable, but validation requires the studies we describe. We
do not claim universal correctness, nor do we propose model-level architectural modications. The
framework addresses text-based human-LLM interaction; other modalities are outside scope.
2 Related Work
The Collaboration Protocol addresses a gap at the intersection of ve established research streams.
2.1 Human-Automation Interaction
Foundational work on levels of automation [Sheridan & Verplank, 1978, Parasuraman et al., 2000]
established taxonomies for allocating tasks between humans and machines. These frameworks address who does what but not who decides whatthe semantic authority question central to our
work.
Parasuraman & Riley [1997] identied patterns of automation misuse, disuse, and abuse, demonstrating that failures often stem from inappropriate trust calibration rather than capability mismatch. Our Intent Control Degree construct extends this insight by providing a mechanism for
users to calibrate trust through explicit control over ambiguity resolution.
Endsley [2017] identies situation awareness as a key challenge in automation. Silent Intent
Inference may degrade situation awareness by resolving ambiguity invisiblyusers cannot be aware
of decisions they were never shown.
Gap: Existing frameworks focus on task allocation but do not address semantic authority.
2.2 Shared Mental Models
Research on shared mental models [Mathieu et al., 2000, Cannon-Bowers & Salas, 2001] demonstrates that team performance depends on aligned understanding. Human-AI teams face a unique
challenge: the AI's mental model is opaque and cannot be directly inspected.
The Collaboration Protocol addresses this by externalizing the points at which human and AI
understanding may diverge (Intent Pivot Points), making implicit dierences explicit and actionable.
