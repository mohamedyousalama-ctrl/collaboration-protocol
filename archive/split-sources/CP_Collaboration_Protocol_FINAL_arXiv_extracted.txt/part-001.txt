2.3 Explainable AI
The XAI literature [Miller, 2019, Gunning et al., 2019] focuses on making AI reasoning transparent
after decisions are made. This post-hoc transparency is valuable but insucientby the time an
explanation is oered, the silent inference has already occurred.
Bansal et al. [2021] demonstrate that explanations improve human-AI team performance only
when appropriately calibrated. Lai & Tan [2019] show that explanations alone do not guarantee
appropriate reliance. These ndings support our argument that transparency mechanisms must be
embedded in the interaction protocol itself.
Gap: XAI explains AI decisions but does not prevent unauthorized decision-making.

2.4 Trust in Automation
Lee & See [2004] establish that appropriate trust calibration depends on understanding automation
capabilities. Overtrust leads to automation bias; undertrust leads to disuse.
The Collaboration Protocol provides a mechanism for dynamic trust calibration: users adjust
Intent Control Degree based on evolving assessment of AI reliability. This operationalizes the

Gap: Trust research identies the calibration problem but provides limited mechanisms for

2.5 Prompt Engineering
Work on prompt engineering [Liu et al., 2023, White et al., 2023] and conversational interface design
[Clark et al., 2019] attempts to reduce ambiguity through better input formulation. We argue this
addresses a symptom (ambiguous inputs) rather than the underlying cause (unauthorized inference).
Even well-crafted prompts contain residual ambiguity; the question is how systems handle it.
Gap: Prompt engineering treats ambiguity as an input problem; we treat it as a protocol
problem.
2.6 Current LLM Mitigation Mechanisms
Contemporary LLM systems have developed partial mechanisms for handling ambiguity, which we
review to contextualize our contribution.
Clarication Questions. Modern conversational AI systems sometimes ask clarifying questions when facing ambiguous inputs. Claude's approach to ambiguity is inuenced by Anthropic's
core design of helpfulness and honesty, with research showing that Claude's default behavior is
to avoid unfounded speculation. When faced with ambiguous or sensitive requests, Claude 4 asks
follow-up questions instead of guessing, reducing misunderstandings. However, these clarications
are inconsistenttriggered by model uncertainty thresholds rather than user-specied control, and
focused on preventing errors rather than preserving semantic authority.
Chain-of-Thought Reasoning. OpenAI o1 thinks before it answersit can produce a long
internal chain of thought before responding to the user. This approach makes reasoning more
transparent but does not address the core issue: OpenAI forbids users from trying to reveal o1's
chain of thought, which is hidden by design. Even when visible, chain-of-thought reveals how the
model reasoned, not whether it had authority to make the semantic choices it made.
Conversational Repair Research. HCI research has extensively studied breakdown recovery
in chatbot interactions. Providing options and explanations were generally favored, as they manifest
initiative from the chatbot and are actionable to recover from breakdowns. When chatbots are
deployed to automate customer service, it is nearly inevitable that situations will arise in which they
struggle to understand customer requests. Unfortunately, the onus of resolving such conversational
breakdowns tends to fall on either the customer or the chatbot alone. This research focuses on
recovering from misunderstandings rather than preventing unauthorized semantic decisions.
Why These Mechanisms Remain Insucient. Current approaches share three limitations
that CP addresses:
1. Reactive, not proactive: Clarications occur when the model detects uncertainty, not when
users request control over semantic decisions.
2. Binary, not continuous: Systems either ask or don't askusers cannot specify their preferred
level of involvement (the ICD contribution).

2.7 Agentic Systems and Multi-Turn Interaction
Recent advances in LLM agents introduce additional considerations for intent preservation. Yao et
al. [2023] present ReAct, which synergizes reasoning and acting by interleaving reasoning traces with
task-specic actions. While ReAct improves interpretability by making reasoning visible, it does not
address who authorized the semantic choices embedded in those reasoning traces. Similarly, Shinn et
al. [2023] introduce Reexion, where agents verbally reect on task feedback and maintain episodic
memory to improve decision-making across trials. This self-improvement mechanism enhances task
success but operates after intent interpretation has occurredthe agent reects on outcomes, not
on whether its interpretation of user intent was authorized.
These agentic approaches share a common limitation from CP's perspective: they optimize for
task completion rather than semantic authority. An agent using ReAct or Reexion may become
highly eective at achieving goals, but those goals reect the agent's interpretation of user intent,
not necessarily the user's actual intent. As agents become more autonomous and operate across
extended interactions, the risk of accumulated silent inferences compounds. CP addresses this orthogonal dimension by surfacing intent-level decisions before the agent commits to an interpretation,
regardless of how sophisticated its subsequent reasoning or reection may be.
